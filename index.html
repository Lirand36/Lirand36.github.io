How I think

Six principles that show up in every project. Each links to where you can see it in practice.

  1. 01

    Every system has a buyer

    Before I build, I name the person and the one decision it should change: which three deals to fight for this quarter, which visitor to walk over to right now. If it doesn't change a decision or an action, it doesn't ship.

    Seen in: Deal Intel, Live Event Lead Engine

  2. 02

    A sharp question beats a big model

    “Which accounts should we expand?” hides five decisions: what counts as expansion, which quarter, measured how. Pinning those down first makes the build smaller and the number defensible in front of the board.

    Seen in: Quarterly Expansion Plan from Messy Data

  3. 03

    AI reads the mess, rules handle the money

    Models are great at messy job titles and misspelled company names. Anything that becomes a dollar figure, an owner or a lifecycle stage stays in plain, testable rules, because that's what finance and sales leadership will audit.

    Seen in: Live Event Lead Engine, Quarterly Expansion Plan from Messy Data

  4. 04

    A missed lead is cheaper than a wrong account

    When the system isn't sure, it says so and a person decides. A bad merge or a misrouted executive costs trust that takes quarters to rebuild. A lead held for review costs a few minutes.

    Seen in: Quarterly Expansion Plan from Messy Data, Dedupe Tool, Live Event Lead Engine

  5. 05

    Pipeline you can't defend isn't pipeline

    If the data can't support a forecast, I don't produce one. I name the gap, the owner who can close it and a date, so leadership plans on numbers that hold up.

    Seen in: Quarterly Expansion Plan from Messy Data, Deal Intel

  6. 06

    Adoption is the real KPI

    A score nobody trusts is a score nobody uses. Every number shows its parts and a one-line reason, so reps can question it, believe it and act on it.

    Seen in: Deal Intel, Revenue Team Hub, Dedupe Tool

About

I've sat in three seats at the revenue table.

In-house at a tech company, on the consulting side, and as an in-house technical project manager. Each seat showed me the same revenue machine from a different angle. Now I build GTM systems that remove manual friction so quietly that teams stop noticing it was ever there.

Let's talk on LinkedIn ↗
  1. 01

    In-house, at a tech company

    Sitting next to sales leadership taught me that the data to win more deals usually exists already. It's just scattered across tools nobody has time to connect. My job there was to turn it into decisions and daily actions.

  2. 02

    On the consulting side

    Delivering CRM projects for many clients taught me scoping, risk and documentation, and that a system only works if non-technical people actually adopt it. I design for the person using it on a busy Tuesday.

  3. 03

    In-house, as a technical project manager

    Owning a CRM end to end taught me that the details are the job: routing, integrations, clean data. When they work, nobody notices. When they break, the whole revenue team feels it.
