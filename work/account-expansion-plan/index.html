Quarterly Expansion Plan from Messy Data

An executive asked which accounts to expand next quarter and what it's worth. The data came from four systems that don't agree. I answered with five plays, each with an owner and a dollar figure, and said plainly what the data can't answer.

The business problem

A revenue leader asks a deliberately vague question:

“Which accounts should we prioritize for expansion next quarter, and how much pipeline could that be worth?”

The data was four exports that don’t join cleanly: the account list, the deals, marketing leads with company names typed by hand, and monthly product usage. The work had a hard 4-hour cap, and a clear partial answer was explicitly worth more than a complete one that ran over.

How I read the requirements

Before writing any code I spent most of the time box on the question itself. About 75–80% thinking, 20–25% building.

First I made the question specific:

Word in the question What I decided it means
Expansion Existing customers buying more: upsells and new deals. Renewing the same contract is not expansion.
Next quarter Q1, January to March
Worth Each play’s dollar figure says what it measures (a price gap, prior contract value, usage value, money at risk). They measure different things, so they are never added up.
Prioritize Named accounts, an owner and a next action, not a ranked score

Then I shaped the answer for the person asking. A revenue leader needs three things: business logic they can trust, where the revenue is, and the decisions only a leader can make (retention, sales effort, data fixes).

Key decisions and trade-offs

1. Plays, not a predictive score. The obvious answer was to score every account on how likely it is to expand. I tested that with a backtest: rebuild the score at each month end using only what was known on that date, then check who actually opened an expansion deal in the next 90 days. Result: AUC 0.60, where 0.5 is a coin flip, on only 15 accounts that expanded. Leads on their own scored 0.52. That is too weak to forecast from, so I built the plan on plays the data supports directly, and made Q1 the test.

2. Five plays in three groups.

Group Play Owner What the $ measures
Act now Price to usage Sales $1.6M a year: customers paying less per active user than similar customers
Act now Land and expand Customer Success 12 customers landed in the last 12 months, not expanded yet
Validate first Q1 expansion window Sales $2.6M prior contract value, not expansion value
Validate first Recover revenue RevOps + Finance $3.1M usage value from customers using the product with no contract on record
Protect Revenue protection Account owners + CRM architect $7.5M paid so far by customers showing no usage

3. Two assumptions the leader can change live. Who we compare price with (a typical customer in the segment, or the cheaper ones) and who counts as a new customer (signed in the last 6, 12 or 24 months). Both recalculate on the page, using the same rules as the pipeline. Comparing with cheaper customers drops “price to usage” from $1.6M to $380k, and that is shown, not hidden.

4. AI in exactly one place. 150 of 900 lead names had typos or different company endings. Code can’t tell a typo from a different company; a language model can. So AI matches messy lead names to accounts, and nowhere else: no AI where a number becomes money, and no AI in the data-safety checks.

How I handled risk

The AI component, measured against a hand-checked answer list:

  • 148 of 150 messy names decided correctly; 81 of 81 non-customers kept out, look-alikes included
  • 0 leads tied to the wrong customer. Plain name similarity found only 60, or tied 12 to the wrong customer when loosened.
  • Unsure means “no match”: the worst case is a missed lead, never a wrong account.
  • Ask twice: the two answers agree on 149 of 150 today. A drop means something changed, which is how I’d know it’s wrong three months from now with nobody complaining.
  • A person decides the unsure ones, and the plan doesn’t rebuild until they have. Each decision is saved and reused.
  • Fallbacks: three backup models tried in order; if all fail, those leads stay unmatched and retry next run. One setting turns the AI off entirely.

What I refused to answer:

  • “How much pipeline is it worth?” The export has no open deals and no targets. So each play shows its own figure, never a total, never a forecast.
  • Are the 19 customers without a contract using the product for free? The data can’t tell free use from a missing deal. Flagged, not claimed as lost revenue.
  • Who will expand on their own? Tested: barely better than a coin flip.

Data-quality memo: every problem got a patch now, a fix at the source, an owner and a date. For example: 21 accounts with no product link (19 of them paying), usage with no contract on record, outbound leads that wrongly carry a marketing-qualified date, and currency labels that don’t add up.

Architecture

4 raw exports
  → clean + data checks (exact rules, logged step by step)
  → lead ↔ account matching: code first, then AI, then a person
  → account × month signals
  → plays (owner, next step, $ figure)
  → plan page (two live assumptions)

The data model is built to answer the question every month: one row per account per month, deals saved monthly so a lost renewal stays visible, and a lead ↔ account table that records who decided each match (code, AI or person) and how sure.

How I used AI to build it

Claude Code built the pipeline, one approved step at a time. Gemini runs the matching inside the pipeline. I set up a separate model as a “CRO reviewer”: a devil’s advocate that reviewed every version as the executive would.

What I caught that the AI got wrong:

  • It offered a statistical forecast (~$54k of pipeline) as the answer. I had it tested: 0.60, barely better than a coin flip.
  • It scored every marketing touch. The data didn’t support it, so only demo requests are shown, as a signal, not scored.
  • It read blank qualified dates on outbound leads as missing data. The blank is correct; the error is the outbound leads that do have one.
  • It claimed the plan would be pushed into the CRM as tasks. Nothing like that was built, so I had it removed.

Outcome

A plan that runs from the four raw files with one command, recalculates live when an assumption changes, and gives a leader five decisions with owners, instead of one number nobody can defend.

What I’d do next

  • Pull open deals and renewal dates from the CRM, so Q1 pipeline coverage becomes a real number.
  • Pull loss reasons for the 13 lost renewals ($1.9M) to know what to fix.
  • Run the plan monthly, and each quarter check whether last quarter’s plays actually expanded.
