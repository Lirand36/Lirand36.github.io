The problem

Most forecasts multiply deal value by a stage probability. But a $20M deal never lands at $16M. It lands at $20M, or at zero.

Traditional forecasting relies heavily on CRM stages, probability assumptions and rep judgment. The result is a number that looks precise but often says little about what the customer has actually committed to.

What I built

An evidence-based forecasting engine that reads customer emails and call transcripts, extracts proof of commercial progress using AI, and applies deterministic business rules to determine where each deal stands on its path to signature.

Instead of asking what stage a rep selected, it asks what the customer actually said.

Left out, on purpose

Rep overrides that blend evidence with optimism.

No manually adjusted probabilities disguised as customer commitment. The system separates extracted evidence from the rules that determine forecast status.

The principle behind it

AI reads the mess. Rules handle the money.

The result

Every number in the forecast points back to something a customer actually said.

A transparent forecasting model where leadership can inspect the evidence behind a deal, challenge the reasoning and distinguish genuine buying progress from pipeline optimism.

The full case study

The business problem

A typical B2B forecast takes each deal’s CRM stage, multiplies the amount by a stage probability, and lets the rep override it. Two things go wrong:

  • A stage is a label the seller sets. It says little about whether this customer will sign this quarter.
  • The override mixes evidence with hope. When the number misses, nobody can say why.

For a company selling large AI deployments to enterprises, the gap is bigger. Deals run through security reviews, pilots with measurable targets, procurement and legal. A handful of big deals decides the quarter, and 80% of a $20M deal is $16M, while the deal itself lands at $20M or $0. Whether it signs this quarter is a question of which steps are done and how long the rest take.

How I read the requirements

The spec was long and precise, so before building anything I wrote a readiness note: every place where the spec could be read two ways, with a proposed reading and why. The business owner approved or changed each one, and every decision was recorded with its reasoning. The spec’s 61 acceptance tests became the test plan.

The product had to serve four people with one number:

Who Their question What they see first
CRO What’s the number, and how much should I trust it? System forecast, range and gap to target
VP Sales Where do my managers disagree with the evidence? Judgement gap per manager
Sales manager What changed on my deals, and can I push back? Their deals and moves, also from chat
RevOps What’s waiting for review? Evidence to approve and disputes

Same number for everyone; only the to-do list changes.

Key decisions and trade-offs

1. Time, not probability. Every deal has a short list of steps the customer must complete before signing: sponsor, budget, validation, pilot, security, vendor selection, legal. That list is the deal’s Signature Path. Instead of “60% likely”, the forecast asks two questions: is the customer committed, and with the open steps at a slow, standard or fast pace, does the signature land before quarter end? The answers place the deal in a group, and the group sets the number.

2. A range, not just a number. Conservative (signed + firm), system forecast (+ deals that sign at a normal pace), best case (+ deals that need a fast pace or a vendor decision). One number is what the CRO reports; the width of the range is how much to trust it. A wide range means the quarter rests on a few undecided deals, which is exactly where the work is.

3. Managers’ judgement gets its own number. Managers know things the evidence doesn’t show yet. They can move a deal into a separate Managers’ Commit, but only by betting that named steps will be done by a date, and only if those steps would really make the deal count. The system checks every bet daily. The result is two honest numbers side by side, and the gap between them is where the forecast call should spend its time.

4. Bad news fast, good news checked. A hold or a contrary signal counts the moment it arrives. Good news waits for a reviewer. The forecast errs toward surprises on the upside, never the downside.

5. Planning standards are versioned. How long security review or legal takes, per segment, is set by RevOps in dated versions. A change of rules is visible in the history, and the weekly bridge names “planning standard changed” as its own cause.

6. What I chose not to do. No stage probabilities. No AI score deciding the number, because a model’s confidence can’t be audited by a CFO. No manual email pasting: a forecast that depends on someone remembering to paste is not a system. No silent overrides.

Where AI fits: it reads, rules decide, people approve

The hardest input to a forecast is unstructured: emails and, above all, call transcripts. That’s where a language model is strong, so that’s where it sits. It reads each synced message and returns structured evidence: which step it’s about, whether it’s progress, done or a hold, and the exact quote that supports it.

  • Only the customer’s words count. Transcripts are split by speaker; what our seller says (“so you’ll sign by March?”) is kept as context but never moves a step.
  • The model never sets the number. It proposes evidence; rules a CFO can read decide the group.
  • Every claim carries a quote, so a hallucination is visible: the sentence is either in the email or transcript, or it isn’t.
  • Every evidence record carries its extraction version, so a model upgrade can be replayed against frozen weeks before it goes live.

How I handled risk

Risk What I built
The number changes for no reason The forecast engine is pure. A test fails the build if any engine file reads the clock, randomness, storage, network or environment. Same data, same day, same number.
History gets rewritten Events, evidence, decisions, runs and the change log are append-only. A property test fires random writes and checks that frozen weekly runs still hash the same.
Money drifts through rounding Amounts are integer cents in their own type; rounding happens only on screen
The same email counted twice Sync is idempotent: message ID first, then a content hash
The person who benefits decides Who may approve, reject or move a deal is checked in the application layer, not just hidden in the UI. Reversing confirmed evidence is reserved for the VP Sales, with a reason, and shows in the review log and the weekly bridge.
Nobody trusts the method A backtest scores the system and the managers on closed quarters: precision, capture and timing bias. Trust is earned with data.

Rolling it out without overriding anyone

The system never overrides people. One quarter in shadow mode next to the current forecast, with no process change. The VP Sales sees the full picture first and uses the gap to run the forecast call. At quarter end, the backtest scores both methods. Reps type nothing new, and a manager who beats the system gets the credit.

Architecture

CRM sync: emails · call transcripts · pilot readings
  → AI extraction (versioned) → evidence with quote
  → review: good news needs a person, bad news counts now
  → pure engine: Signature Path × planning standards → group
  → weekly run, frozen → forecast · range · bridge · backtest
  → views per role · actions from chat

Dependencies point inward: everything depends on the engine, and the engine depends on nothing.

Outcome

A working app with a CRM area and a forecast area, five simulated roles, and manager actions from chat (report news, move a deal, undo, and see the effect on the number at once). All 61 acceptance tests in the spec are traced to automated tests: 227 unit, integration and property tests, plus a browser run that clicks through the app, on every change.
